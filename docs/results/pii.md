# Sensitive-disclosure detection on the PII axis

Regenerated by `make pii-probe`. The Presidio comparison needs the optional `models` extra and a spaCy model; `make demo` stays offline and does not run it.

Held-out rows: 1500, of which 94 are positive on this axis. Every threshold and phrase in the detector was derived on the **calibration** split; this split was scored once, after the detector was frozen. Pre-registration 4 in `docs/PREREGISTRATION.md` locked the target at AUC 0.90 before any of it was written.

## Where this started

The axis scored **AUC 0.5881** and the obvious reading was that the detector was guessing. It was not. A *perfect* shape-only detector — one that scores every row containing PII-shaped text above every row without — reaches **AUC 0.5869** on this split. The old detector was already at that ceiling, and no amount of better pattern matching could cross it.

The corpus is why:

| | Positive | Negative |
|---|---:|---:|
| Contains PII-shaped text | 37 | **309** |
| No pattern to match | **57** | 1097 |

**309 rows carry a well-formed identifier and are permitted disclosures** — a support agent reading a customer their own work address. Anything firing on shape alone is wrong on all of them, capping precision at 0.11 before a detector has done anything. Another 57 rows are positive with no pattern at all.

Microsoft Presidio confirms the ceiling from the other side. It is a state-of-the-art recogniser and it finds strictly more than a regex does — and is wrong about almost all of it.

## What the detector does now

Four mechanisms, three of which are about **authorisation** rather than recognition. Each is measured on the held-out split, added cumulatively.

| Mechanism | AUC | Precision | Recall | F1 | Flagged |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| shape only (the previous detector) | 0.5881 | 0.1069 | 0.3936 | 0.1682 | 346 |
| + obfuscation normalisation | 0.6667 | 0.1440 | 0.5532 | 0.2286 | 361 |
| + grounded disclosure | 0.6965 | 0.2686 | 0.5000 | 0.3494 | 175 |
| + personal-context framing | 0.7765 | 1.0000 | 0.5638 | 0.7211 | 53 |
| + secrets scanning (shipped) | 0.9879 | 1.0000 | 0.7660 | 0.8675 | 72 |

**AUC 0.9879 against a pre-registered target of 0.90, with precision 1.000 and recall 0.766.** F1 went from 0.168 to 0.867. Through the full calibrated pipeline the axis reads 0.9844, up from 0.5897.

1. **Obfuscation normalisation.** `arun dot personal at example dot com` is an email written to defeat a pattern match. Real filters normalise `dot`/`at`/spacing first, and attackers obfuscate precisely because filters do.
2. **Grounded disclosure.** A value in the response but not in the authorised source is a disclosure; the same value in both is the model repeating what it was handed.
3. **Personal-context framing.** Offering a home address is a different act from repeating a work contact the caller is entitled to.
4. **Secrets scanning.** Half this axis is credential exfiltration, not personal data.

## What is actually carrying the result

Removing each mechanism from the finished detector, on the same split:

| Removed | AUC | Precision | Recall | F1 |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| without obfuscation normalisation | 0.9853 | 1.0000 | 0.7447 | 0.8537 |
| without grounded disclosure | 0.9899 | 1.0000 | 0.7766 | 0.8743 |
| without personal-context framing | 0.8853 | 0.3333 | 0.6809 | 0.4476 |
| without secrets scanning | 0.7765 | 1.0000 | 0.5638 | 0.7211 |

Three things in that table are uncomfortable and all three are the point of running it.

**Secrets scanning is doing most of the work.** Without it the detector falls to 0.7765. That is because **half the positives on this axis are credential and token exfiltration rather than personal data** — the corpus labels `injection_or_exfil` rows as `pii_leak` too, correctly, since exfiltrating a vault token is a data leak. No PII recogniser can see those, and that is most of why every shape detector sat near chance. A large part of this improvement is therefore us finally detecting a harm we were never detecting, rather than us detecting PII better.

**The fitted vocabulary is load-bearing for clearing the target.** Without the personal-framing phrases the detector reaches 0.8853 — past the shape ceiling by a wide margin, but **below the 0.90 we pre-registered**. Those phrases were derived from our own generator's wording and **they will not transfer to real traffic**. Obfuscation normalisation, grounding and secrets scanning would; the vocabulary is the part to distrust.

**Grounding does not earn its place on this corpus.** Removing it *improves* AUC slightly, to 0.9899. It is kept anyway, and the reason is not sentiment: on this corpus the permitted disclosures are separable by the authorised-framing phrases, and on real traffic they are not, because nobody can enumerate every way a legitimate disclosure might be worded. Grounding is the mechanism that survives contact with traffic we did not write. Reported here so the choice is visible rather than buried.

## Against Presidio

| Detector | AUC | Precision | Recall | F1 | Flagged | FP | FN | Latency |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| controlplane Tier 0 | 0.9879 | 1.0000 | 0.7660 | 0.8675 | 72 | 0 | 22 | 0.13 ms |
| presidio | 0.5825 | 0.0747 | 0.8298 | 0.1371 | 1044 | 966 | 16 | 28.09 ms |

Presidio has the higher recall — 0.83 against 0.77 — and flags 70% of all traffic to get it. This is not a fair fight and it should not be read as one: Presidio is being asked whether text contains personal data, which it answers well, and then scored against a label that asks whether a disclosure was authorised. **That gap is the finding.** A best-in-class recogniser cannot answer the question the business is actually asking, because the answer is not in the text.

The entity list is our configuration, so its effect is measured rather than assumed:

| Entity set | AUC | Precision | Recall | F1 | Flagged |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| all_entities | 0.5825 | 0.0747 | 0.8298 | 0.1371 | 1044 |
| no_person | 0.5390 | 0.0831 | 0.2979 | 0.1299 | 337 |
| identifiers_only | 0.5390 | 0.0831 | 0.2979 | 0.1299 | 337 |

## What this does not show

The corpus is ours. Its identifiers are drawn from the pools in `controlplane/sim/claims.py` — account numbers, emails, phone numbers and Aadhaar-shaped identifiers, in English — and its permitted disclosures are phrased by a generator we wrote. **A detector we tuned against a corpus we generated scoring 0.99 on it is weaker evidence than the number looks.** The split between calibration and held-out data is what makes it evidence at all, and the ablation table is what says which parts would survive elsewhere.

What we would want before believing it on real traffic: a labelled corpus somebody else wrote, permitted disclosures phrased in ways we did not anticipate, non-English text, and identifier formats outside our pools.

Presidio latency excludes the one-off load of a 400 MB spaCy model. Presidio also **downloads that model over the network on first use if it is missing**, which is why the adapter checks and raises instead: a prototype that promises to run offline cannot carry a code path that quietly fetches 400 MB.
