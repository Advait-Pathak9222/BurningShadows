Executive summary

Every enterprise deploying AI assistants has quietly acquired a new operating cost. Somebody has to check what the model says before it reaches a customer or moves money. That cost is growing faster than the AI spend it protects, and almost nobody is managing it.

The two things organisations do today both waste money. Running a strong checker on every answer is thorough and can cost more than generating the answer did. Sampling one answer in ten is cheap and blind, because it spends the same effort on a question about opening hours as on an instruction to transfer funds.

ControlPlane prices the difference. It estimates what it would cost the business if a given answer were wrong, and buys checking only where the loss prevented is worth more than the check. A safety floor sits underneath that the budget cannot switch off. Everything lands in a tamper evident record that an auditor can recompute.

The finding that shapes this proposal

We set out to save compute. The measurement said something more useful. Between 81 and 98 paise in every assurance rupee is human review time, not machine time. One completed review costs ₹120 against ₹3.20 for the most expensive automated check. Every vendor in this category competes on the 2 to 19 paise and nobody is managing the rest.

That changes what is worth buying. The compute saving is real and it is the smaller prize. The larger prize is deciding which cases reach a person at all, and in what order, because review capacity is fixed and the queue is already oversubscribed.

What we are asking for. One customer facing route, in shadow mode, for eight weeks. No enforcement, no latency risk, no change to the existing model. At the end you will have your own numbers for what assurance costs you today and what it would cost run this way.

Problem framing

The problem is not that AI makes mistakes. It is that checking for mistakes has no budget, no owner and no way to prove it worked.

The cost nobody planned for

An assistant answering customer questions produces thousands of answers a day. A fraction are wrong in ways that matter. Finding them costs money in two places, and the second is much larger than the first.

A review is 37.5 times the most expensive automated check. It follows that once checking escalates even a small share of traffic to people, the people dominate the bill. Our measurement confirms it across every budget setting we tested.

Three failures that follow

Overspending

Checking everything

Adds 900 milliseconds to every answer and, at two million interactions a year, costs ₹64 lakh in compute before a single reviewer is paid.

Underspending

Sampling blindly

A fixed sample cannot tell a question about opening hours from an instruction to move money, so it protects the cheap and misses the expensive.

Unprovable

No defensible record

When a regulator asks why one answer was released, a log line is not an answer. The arithmetic behind the decision is what is needed, and it is usually gone.

The queue is already failing quietly

In our evaluation the review queue is 1.5 times oversubscribed at the tightest budget and up to 3.4 times at full coverage. Keeping up with arrivals needs 3.0 reviewers against the two staffed. Roughly a third of raised cases are shed unreviewed. Most organisations are already shedding like this. They simply do not measure which cases they lose.

The question is not whether cases get dropped. They already are. The question is whether the ones dropped are the cheap ones or the expensive ones.

Business opportunity

The budget for this already exists inside most enterprises. It is sitting in manual review headcount and in unmeasured incident cost, not in a tooling line.

Three things arrive together. AI assistants are moving from pilots into customer facing and money touching workflows. Regulation is starting to ask for evidence of control rather than assertion of it. And the first wave of guardrail tooling has made checking possible without making it affordable, which is exactly the gap a budgeting layer fills.

Proposed solution

A gateway that sits between your application and whatever model answers it, and decides how much checking each answer is worth.

What it does, in the order it does it

StepWhat happens
Refuse earlyA cheap scan reads the prompt before the model runs. An obvious attack is refused there and costs neither generation nor checking.
Score the answerCheap checks score the answer on five kinds of harm for a combined 20 paise, and those scores are converted into probabilities.
Apply the floorEach route has a safety threshold above which a check is compulsory. The budget cannot switch this off.
Price the choiceBelow the floor, the system buys the expensive check only where the loss it would prevent beats what it costs.
Split words from actionsText can be shown while checking runs. Actions that move money or change records wait behind their own gate.
Prioritise peopleWhatever needs a person is queued by value per reviewer minute against the deadline, not first come first served.
Record everythingEvery decision, its inputs and its price go into a chained record where a later edit is detectable.

What integration actually involves

The gateway presents the same interface your application already calls, so integration is a change of base URL rather than a rewrite. It reads only the request, the answer, any supplied context and any proposed actions. It needs no access to model internals, which means it works in front of a model you rent as well as one you host, and it can run entirely inside your own network where data residency requires that.

The commercial consequence of that design. You are not locked to a model vendor, a detector vendor or a cloud. The system needs three numbers about any checker you plug in, which are its cost, its latency and how much it catches. Change the checker and the economics recalculate themselves.

Where it fits first

The strongest early fit is a regulated enterprise running a customer facing assistant alongside an assistant that can take an action. Banking, insurance, telecom and large scale consumer support all have the same shape. High volume, a real consequence table, an existing review desk, and a regulator who will eventually ask for evidence.

Customer use case

Two requests arrive one minute apart at the same support assistant. A fixed rule treats them the same. Here is what happens instead.

Request one

A routine ticket lookup

A customer asks for the status of an open ticket. The cheap checks score the answer well below the safety threshold. The expensive judge would prevent very little extra loss and would cost far more than that, so it is not bought. The answer is released.

Cost of assurance. 20 paise. Delay added. None the customer notices.

Request two

A payment instruction

A request on the finance route carries a transfer, and the model echoes back a secret its source said must never be repeated. Risk lands above the safety threshold, so a check is compulsory before any price is considered. The judge is bought, the answer is blocked, the transfer is denied, and the case is queued for a person.

Cost of assurance. ₹3.20. Loss on the table. ₹69,357.

The point of the pair. Neither request was treated as average. The first was cheap because nothing was at stake. The second was expensive because a great deal was, and the system could afford it precisely because the first one did not spend.

What the customer never sees

In the second case the customer receives a message saying the request has been passed to a colleague. They do not see the model's original answer. The business does not lose the money, and the review desk receives a case tagged with what it is worth, so it is worked before the routine ones that arrived earlier.

The two baselines a customer already pays

Doing nothing. With no verification, residual loss on the same traffic is 4.29 times what allocation leaves. The customer's equivalent is their own incident rate multiplied by their own consequence range, which a two week study produces.

Blanket manual review. Reviewing every flagged case at current flag rates is what the oversubscription describes. Most organisations are already shedding. They do not measure which cases they lose.

Stated plainly, because it is the strongest objection. At the tightest budget a well tuned blanket cheap check averts 2.4% more than we do for a fraction of the compute spend. Our advantage on compute alone is small, and once both policies are charged for the reviewer minutes they generate it is smaller still. The compute ratio in that baseline's favour falls from 1.82 times to 1.035 times once attention is counted, and that happens because the attention bill dwarfs compute for both, not because allocation improved. Where we beat the obvious alternative is on the resource that costs the money.

Eight weeks, one support route and one route that can take an action. Nothing is enforced until week seven, and nothing is enforced at all without approval.

Promotion to production requires all of these

  • Allocation beats the fixed rate baseline at equal spend across agreed budget bands, with intervals rather than point estimates.
  • The route release bound stays under the approved threshold on fresh labels.
  • Every proposed action has an audit record, and the record sink meets your retention requirements.
  • Added latency at the 99th percentile stays inside the route service level objective.
  • Decisions stay stable across the approved consequence ranges.
  • The incident and override owners have approved the runbook.

Current validation boundaries

The prototype is evaluated on synthetic traffic with configured consequence prices, and on five public corpora. That is enough to demonstrate the mechanism and its economics. It is not a customer result, and the following would have to close before production.

  • Calibration is fitted on synthetic traffic and does not transfer. Roughly 500 labelled rows per route, about four weeks of one reviewer, would refit it.
  • The expensive judge is a deterministic stand in. The adapter exists and two real adapters are written against it, but a production judge must be built and its catch rate re measured.
  • Drift is not detected today. The stated bound holds only for the distribution it was calibrated on.
  • The effect gate decides and records but does not hold a lock across a crash. Irreversible actions need a durable lease before enforcement.
  • The record store assumes a single process. Multiple workers need shared state.

Closing recommendation

Approve an eight week shadow pilot on one customer facing route, with no enforcement.

The reason to start in shadow mode is that it costs almost nothing and it settles the argument with your own data. In two weeks you will know what assurance actually costs you today, split between compute and people, which most organisations cannot currently state. In eight weeks you will know whether allocating that spend beats what you do now on your traffic, and you will have a release bound measured on your own labels rather than on ours.

We will also tell you if the answer is no. Allocation helps under conditions that are computable in advance, and it does not help outside them. On one of the five public corpora we tested, it won on only one budget setting out of seven, and we report that alongside the results where it won. A pilot that concludes the honest answer is a fixed rate policy on your traffic is a successful pilot, and it will have cost you eight weeks and no enforcement risk to find out.

The ask. One route. One shadow pilot. Eight weeks. At the end you own the numbers, the calibration fitted to your traffic, and a decision you can defend to a regulator.
